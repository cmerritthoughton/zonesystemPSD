# Training guide

This guide covers what Adams was doing, why it carries over to drawing, and how to practise it with the panel. The goal is the skill, not the software: once you've built the habit, you should be able to visualize, predict and place values without the panel.

## What Adams actually did

**1. Visualization comes first.** In 1927, on Half Dome and down to his last plates, Adams realized that an accurate exposure wouldn't carry what he felt about the cliff. He used a deep red filter, which turned the sky nearly black, and later called *Monolith* his first true visualization. Decide what the image should feel like before you measure, even if that departs from what the eye sees.
*In the panel:* the Visualize step. Training mode won't reveal anything until you've written the print you want.

**2. Place one value and let the rest fall.** You don't set every tone. Choose the value that matters most, usually the darkest shadow that must keep its texture (Zone III), put it where you want it, and let everything else follow. For *Moonrise, Hernandez* Adams had no meter; he remembered how bright the moon was and placed it on Zone VII.
*In the panel:* marker #1 is your shadow anchor. Interpret ▸ Place puts it on the zone you choose and reports the exposure shift.

**3. The tonal range is a budget.** The rule was "expose for the shadows, develop for the highlights". Exposure sets the floor and development sets the ceiling. Adams expanded flat scenes (N+) and compressed harsh ones (N−) so the scene fit the print he had visualized.
*In the panel:* the N−2 … N+2 buttons, or let marker #2 fall on a zone and read the N you chose.

**4. Separation, not contrast.** Adams cared most that neighbouring values stay distinguishable, especially II/III and VII/VIII. There are two failures: mud (everything in the middle) and chalk and soot (only black and white).
*In the panel:* the Textured darks, Textured lights, Middle and No texture overlays, plus the separation readings on Reveal.

**5. Luminosity is a relationship.** Light reads as light when the bright areas are small against the dark masses, when they're approached through graded near-highlights, and when the darks around them still have texture. Adams burned skies and print edges almost every time.
*In the panel:* Burn edges and Burn sky.

**6. Light over local colour.** His filters overrode an object's own value so that light, not local colour, organized the picture.
*In the panel:* the Filter list, and the Opportunities sheet rows.

**7. The score and the performance.** Adams printed *Moonrise* for decades, and the later prints are far darker than the early ones. Your reference photo is the score, and your drawing is a performance of it.
*In the panel:* the Opportunities sheet, which shows twenty performances of one score.

## The ink translation

How light a hatched area looks isn't proportional to how much paper you cover. Optically averaged (lines fine enough, or far enough away, to merge), with paper reflecting about 85% and ink about 2%:

| Zone | ≈ ink coverage | Natural approach |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 100% | Solid black, as a designed shape |
| I | ~98% | Solid with the barest breaks |
| II | ~95% | White-line work; hatching around white |
| III | ~90% | Four crosshatch layers; paper flecks must survive |
| IV | ~83% | Three crosshatch layers |
| V | ~75% | Two dense layers |
| VI | ~65% | Two lighter layers |
| VII | ~50% | One layer, line as wide as the gap |
| VIII | ~35% | Sparse hatching, stipple, broken line |
| IX | ~20% | A few tapered marks |
| X | 0% | Paper, as a designed shape |

Three consequences:

- **The dark half of the scale lives in the last quarter of ink coverage.** Telling II from III is the difference between about 5% and 10% of paper showing through.
- **Most drawings are lighter than their artist thinks.** With lines as thick as their gaps, one layer ≈ Zone VII, two ≈ V–VI, three ≈ III–IV and four ≈ II.
- **For the dark zones, think in white lines:** scratchboard, white ink, or hatching around reserved light.

*In the panel:* Ink ▸ Measure from markers sets the scale from your own ink and paper. **Blur a copy** shows the zones your marks actually produce, and **Read markers** estimates the coverage at each marker.

## Practice, by effort

### Every session (minutes)

1. **Write the sentence first.** Say what the light is doing and what it should feel like, and name your two anchors.
2. **Predict before you reveal.** Guess the zones at #1 and #2 and the dark share. Keep training mode on.
3. **Read your bias on Reveal.** Most people see shadows lighter than they measure, because the eye adapts to shadow and film and paper don't.
4. **Check the four-value and light/shadow overlays.** If the masses don't carry the drama, rendering won't add it.

### Weekly (an hour)

5. **Re-light a reference.** Use Interpret to try red against blue against no filter, and N−1 against N+2. Then make an Opportunities sheet and choose deliberately.
6. **Spend the range.** On the zone map, find what the subject is losing to the background. Compress the unimportant and expand the important.
7. **Burn on purpose.** Darken the sky behind lit forms and darken toward the frame edges, then compare with and without.

### Deep practice (projects)

8. **Calibrate your ink.** Draw an eleven-step wedge on your real paper with your real pens and scan it in Grayscale. Use Ink ▸ Measure from markers on solid ink and bare paper, blur a copy, and read each patch. Repeat at your reproduction size: fine dark textures fill in when reduced.
9. **Study one subject under changing light.** Make five sessions of the same building at different times of day or in different weather, and compare the logs.
10. **Make master transcriptions.** Run an Adams photograph you own through the loop as private study. Predict its zones, reveal them, then try to match its II–IV and VII–IX separations in ink.

## Reading the Reveal

| Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Average miss | Mean absolute difference between your guess and the meter, in zones. Under 0.5 is good, and under 0.3 is excellent. |
| Bias | Positive means you see values lighter than they measure; negative means darker. |
| Dark share | Fraction of the frame in Zones 0–IV. |
| Key | Low if at least 60% is in 0–IV, high if at least 60% is in VI–X, otherwise middle. |
| Range | Full scale needs at least 0.2% of the frame in both 0–I and IX–X. |
| Textured darks / lights | The share of the frame in II–III and VII–VIII, where texture survives at the ends of the scale. |
| Middle | IV–VI, the range where separation turns to mud if too much lives there. |
| No texture | 0–I plus IX–X: pure tone with no detail. |
